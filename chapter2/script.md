## Scene 1: Intro

1. Hi! Today we are doing Chapter 2 of Science Part 1: Periodic Classification of Elements. Many students think this chapter is just a big table to memorise. It is not. Once your basics are clear, the whole table makes sense, almost like a story.
2. Here is our plan. First, we refresh the basics from earlier classes. Then we see how scientists tried to arrange the elements: Dobereiner, Newlands and Mendeleev. Then the modern periodic table. Then the trends, which is where most of the marks are. And finally, some practice questions.

## Scene 2: Basics 1: Element and atom

1. Basics first. An element is a pure substance made of only one kind of atom. Like hydrogen, oxygen, iron or gold. Today we know 118 elements.
2. Every atom has a nucleus in the centre, which has protons and neutrons. Around the nucleus, electrons move in shells.
3. The number of protons is called the atomic number, written as capital Z. In a neutral atom, the number of electrons is also equal to Z. Remember this, because the modern periodic table is built on atomic number.
4. Atomic mass is roughly protons plus neutrons. The early scientists arranged elements by atomic mass, because the inside of the atom was not yet understood.

## Scene 3: Basics 2: Shells and electronic configuration

1. Shells are named K, L, M and N, starting from the nucleus. A shell can hold at most 2 n squared electrons. So K holds 2, L holds 8, M holds 18, and N holds 32.
2. Writing how the electrons fill the shells is called electronic configuration. Sodium has 11 electrons: 2, 8, 1. Chlorine has 17: 2, 8, 7. Argon has 18: 2, 8, 8.
3. Electrons in the outermost shell are called valence electrons. They decide how an element reacts. Sodium has 1 valence electron and gives it away, so its valency is 1. Chlorine has 7 and needs 1 more, so its valency is also 1. Argon's outer shell is full, so it does not react. Its valency is zero.
4. Keep these three ideas in your pocket: atomic number, electronic configuration, and valence electrons. With these three, you can explain almost every question in this chapter.

## Scene 4: Basics 3: Metals, non-metals, metalloids

1. One more basic: metals and non-metals. Metals tend to lose their valence electrons and form positive ions. That is why we call them electropositive. Sodium, magnesium and iron are metals.
2. Non-metals tend to gain electrons and form negative ions. They are electronegative. Chlorine, oxygen and sulphur are non-metals.
3. And some elements behave a bit like both. These are called metalloids. For example boron, silicon, germanium and arsenic.

## Scene 5: Why classify elements?

1. Now the chapter begins. Why classify elements at all? Imagine a library with thousands of books thrown on the floor. You would never find anything. Arrange them by subject, and suddenly it is easy.
2. By the eighteen hundreds, scientists knew many elements, and new ones kept being discovered. To study them easily, they tried to group elements with similar properties. Let us see how these attempts improved, step by step.

## Scene 6: Dobereiner's triads (1817)

1. First attempt: Dobereiner, a German scientist, in 1817. He noticed that some elements come in groups of three with similar properties. He called them triads.
2. Here is the magic. Arrange the three in increasing order of atomic mass. The atomic mass of the middle element is about the average of the other two. Lithium is 7 and potassium is 39. 7 plus 39 is 46, divided by 2 is 23. And sodium is 23!
3. Same for calcium, strontium and barium: 40 plus 137 is 177, half is 88.5, and strontium is about 88. And chlorine, bromine, iodine: 35.5 plus 127 is 162.5, half is about 81, and bromine is 80.
4. The limitation: he could find only a few triads among the elements known then. So this method could not classify all the elements. But it gave an important idea: properties and atomic mass are connected.

## Scene 7: Newlands' law of octaves (1866)

1. Next, John Newlands, an English scientist, in 1866. He arranged the known elements in increasing order of atomic mass, starting from hydrogen and ending at thorium, the 56th element.
2. He noticed that every eighth element had properties similar to the first. Just like music! Sa, re, ga, ma, pa, dha, ni, and then sa comes again. So he called it the law of octaves.
3. Look: lithium, then 7 elements later, sodium. Then 7 more, potassium. All three are similar, reactive metals. Fluorine and chlorine are also in one column, and they are similar too.
4. But there were problems. The law worked well only up to calcium. To fit his pattern, he put two elements, cobalt and nickel, in one place, in the same column as fluorine and chlorine, which are totally different. And iron was placed far away from cobalt and nickel, even though they are similar. He also assumed no new elements would be discovered. So the law failed for heavier elements.

## Scene 8: Mendeleev's periodic table (1869)

1. Now the big one: Dmitri Mendeleev, a Russian scientist, in 1869. At that time, 63 elements were known. He wrote each element and its properties on a card, and kept rearranging the cards like a puzzle.
2. He used two things: atomic mass, and chemical properties, especially the formulas of their oxides and hydrides, which means compounds with oxygen and hydrogen.
3. His law, Mendeleev's periodic law, says: properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic masses. Periodic means they repeat after regular intervals. Write this law word for word in the exam.
4. In his table, the vertical columns are called groups, and the horizontal rows are called periods.

## Scene 9: Mendeleev: merits

1. Why was Mendeleev's table so successful? First, he was bold. Where no known element fitted, he left a gap, and said: an element will be discovered here one day.
2. He even predicted their properties, and named them using the Sanskrit word eka, which means one. Eka-boron, eka-aluminium and eka-silicon. Later they were discovered and named scandium, gallium and germanium. Look how close his predictions for gallium were!
3. Second, he corrected the wrong atomic masses of some elements. For example, the atomic mass of beryllium was corrected from 14.09 to 9.4, so it could sit with similar elements.
4. Third, when the noble gases like helium, neon and argon were discovered later, they fitted neatly as a separate zero group, without disturbing the rest of the table.

## Scene 10: Mendeleev: demerits

1. But his table also had some problems. First, the position of hydrogen. Hydrogen is like the alkali metals in some ways, and like the halogens in other ways. For example, hydrogen exists as H two, just like chlorine exists as C L two. So its correct place was not fixed.
2. Second, isotopes. Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different atomic masses, like chlorine 35 and chlorine 37. If the table is based on atomic mass, where do we put them? Mendeleev's table had no answer.
3. Third, the order was broken in some places. Cobalt, with atomic mass 58.9, was placed before nickel, 58.7, just to keep similar elements together.
4. Fourth, atomic mass does not increase in a regular way from one element to the next. So it was impossible to say how many elements could exist between two elements.

## Scene 11: Modern periodic law (Moseley, 1913)

1. In 1913, Henry Moseley, an English scientist, did experiments with X-rays. He showed that the atomic number is the most basic property of an element, more basic than atomic mass.
2. So the law was changed. The modern periodic law says: properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic numbers. Just one word changed, from mass to number, and many problems disappeared.
3. Isotopes have the same atomic number, so they now sit in the same place. And cobalt, atomic number 27, naturally comes before nickel, atomic number 28. No forcing needed.

## Scene 12: The modern periodic table

1. This is the modern periodic table. It has seven horizontal rows, called periods, and eighteen vertical columns, called groups.
2. The table is divided into blocks. Groups 1 and 2 form the s-block. Groups 13 to 18 form the p-block. Groups 3 to 12 in the middle form the d-block. These are called transition elements.
3. The two rows kept separately at the bottom are the f-block: the lanthanides and the actinides, also called inner transition elements. They are kept below only so the table does not become too wide.
4. And see this zig-zag line in the p-block? Metals are on its left side, non-metals on its right side, and the metalloids lie along this line. Most elements are metals.

## Scene 13: Groups, periods and electronic configuration

1. Now connect this to our basics. Elements in the same group have the same number of valence electrons. Lithium is 2, 1. Sodium is 2, 8, 1. Potassium is 2, 8, 8, 1. All have 1 valence electron. That is why they behave alike.
2. Elements in the same period have the same number of shells. Everything in period 2, from lithium to neon, has 2 shells. So, the period number equals the number of shells. As we go from left to right in a period, the valence electrons increase by one each time.
3. Here is a quick trick to find an element's position. Write its electronic configuration. The number of shells gives the period. For groups 1 and 2, the valence electrons give the group. For the p-block, add 10. For example, chlorine is 2, 8, 7. Three shells, so period 3. Seven valence electrons, plus 10, so group 17.
4. The number of elements in the periods is 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32 and 32. This comes from how many electrons the shells can hold.

## Scene 14: Trend 1: Valency

1. Now the trends. These are asked a lot in the exam. First, valency. Valency depends on valence electrons.
2. Across period 3: sodium 1, magnesium 2, aluminium 3, silicon 4, then phosphorus 3, sulphur 2, chlorine 1, and argon 0. So in a period, valency first increases from 1 to 4, and then decreases to 0.
3. Down a group, valence electrons stay the same, so valency stays the same. All group 1 elements have valency 1.

## Scene 15: Trend 2: Atomic size (atomic radius)

1. Second, atomic size, measured as atomic radius: the distance from the nucleus to the outermost shell. It is tiny, so we measure it in picometres. One picometre is ten to the power minus twelve metre.
2. Across a period, from left to right, atomic radius decreases. Why? The number of shells stays the same, but the atomic number increases, so the positive charge on the nucleus increases. A stronger nucleus pulls the electrons closer. Lithium is 152 picometres, but oxygen is only 66.
3. Down a group, atomic radius increases. That is because a new shell is added at every step. More shells means a bigger atom, even though the nuclear charge also increases. Lithium 152, sodium 186, potassium 231.

## Scene 16: Trend 3: Metallic and non-metallic character

1. Third, metallic character. A metal easily loses its valence electrons. So metallic character means: how easily an atom gives away electrons.
2. Across a period, metallic character decreases. The atom gets smaller and the nucleus holds the electrons more tightly, so giving them away is harder. In period 3, sodium and magnesium are metals, silicon is a metalloid, and phosphorus, sulphur and chlorine are non-metals.
3. Down a group, metallic character increases. The outer electrons are farther from the nucleus, so they are easier to remove. That is why potassium is more reactive than sodium.
4. Non-metallic character is the exact opposite: it increases across a period and decreases down a group. Among the halogens, fluorine is the most reactive, and reactivity decreases down to iodine.

## Scene 18: Memory tricks

1. Some memory tricks. For the history, remember: 3, 8, mass, number. Dobereiner's triads had 3 elements. Newlands' octaves had 8. Mendeleev used atomic mass. Moseley used atomic number.
2. For group 2: beryllium, magnesium, calcium, strontium, barium, radium. Remember it as: Beta Mange Car, Scooter Baap Raazi. And for the halogens, fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine: Fat Cats Bring Ice.
